# An Educational Research Agenda for the AI Era: From Authentic Competence to Human Development

*Human Development, Learning and Educational Transformation in the Age of Artificial Intelligence — Research Agenda 2026–2035*

## 1. Educational thought and research questions arising from the VNU interview

The article [Teachers and the changes education needs in the age of AI](https://vnu.edu.vn/nguoi-thay-va-nhung-chuyen-dong-can-thiet-cua-giao-duc-trong-thoi-dai-ai-post40866.html), published on the occasion of Vietnamese Teachers' Day, 20 November 2026, provides a starting point for a research programme that extends beyond an interview about teaching.

The passages retrieved from the original article and its republication on the VNU University of Education website suggest a coherent educational perspective. AI is not merely changing the tools of instruction; it is reopening questions concerning educational purposes, the formation of knowledge, the nature of competence, the validity of assessment and the role of human beings.

The central scientific issue is the distinction between what people can produce with technological assistance and what has genuinely become part of their own capabilities.

Taken seriously, this is not exclusively a problem of educational technology. It lies at the intersection of:

- Philosophy of education and epistemology.
- Cognitive science and the learning sciences.
- Educational psychology and educational measurement.
- Teacher education and professional learning.
- Artificial intelligence and human–computer interaction.
- Economics of education, talent development and social equity.

The proposed programme should extend beyond Pedagogical GenAI Judgment (PGJ). PGJ can serve as a specialised strand concerned with professional judgment within a broader programme of research on human development.

### 1.1. Translating educational ideas into scientific problems

The following table interprets and develops ideas from the interview. It does not represent empirical findings established by the interview itself.

| Educational idea | Scientific problem |
|---|---|
| A high-quality product does not necessarily indicate high competence | Validity of inferences from learning products to individual competence |
| Learners should remain able to solve problems when assistance is withdrawn | Internalisation, retention and transfer |
| Assessment should examine performance both with and without AI | Measurement of competence across conditions of assistance |
| Teachers must evaluate and select AI recommendations | Professional judgment, agency and accountability |
| Learners should formulate questions and examine counterexamples | Epistemic agency and generative thinking |
| Talent identification should not rely solely on current achievement | Dynamic assessment, developmental potential and learning capacity |
| Technology should serve each learner's circumstances and needs | Pedagogical personalisation and equitable learning opportunities |
| Teachers should attend to students' long-term development | Pedagogical relationships, motivation and holistic development |
| Time should be used purposefully | Allocation of cognitive resources, learning time and professional well-being |

### 1.2. Overarching scientific question

> In a world where artificial intelligence can increasingly perform complex intellectual tasks, how should education be designed, organised and assessed so that improvements in technological capability translate into the development of human competence, agency and potential?

This question is broad enough to sustain a long-term interdisciplinary programme, but must be decomposed into specific mechanisms and falsifiable hypotheses.

## 2. Literature review: Major shifts in international educational research

Identifying consequential research gaps requires distinguishing questions that predate generative AI from phenomena that GenAI creates or substantially transforms.

### 2.1. Theoretical foundations

| Theoretical foundation | Representative work | Relevance to the programme |
|---|---|---|
| Educational purposes | Biesta (2009), *Educational Assessment, Evaluation and Accountability* | Education cannot be reduced to measurable outcomes and achievement. |
| Learning versus performance | [Soderstrom & Bjork (2015)](https://doi.org/10.1177/1745691615569000), *Perspectives on Psychological Science* | Immediate performance does not necessarily reflect durable learning. |
| Cognitive offloading | [Risko & Gilbert (2016)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tics.2016.07.002), *Trends in Cognitive Sciences* | Explains how people transfer parts of cognitive activity to external tools. |
| Cognitive engagement (ICAP) | [Chi & Wylie (2014)](https://doi.org/10.1080/00461520.2014.965823), *Educational Psychologist* | Differentiates passive, active, constructive and interactive engagement. |
| Productive failure | [Sinha & Kapur (2021)](https://doi.org/10.3102/00346543211019105), *Review of Educational Research* | Provides a basis for investigating initial problem solving before instruction. |
| Assessment validity | [Kane (2013)](https://doi.org/10.1111/jedm.12000), *Journal of Educational Measurement* | Focuses attention on the validity of inferences from assessment results to competence and educational decisions. |
| Evaluative judgement | [Bearman et al. (2024)](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1438704), *Assessment & Evaluation in Higher Education* | Develops the capacity to judge the quality of products and processes involving AI. |
| Human–AI synergy | [Vaccaro et al. (2024)](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41562-024-02024-1), *Nature Human Behaviour* | Human–AI collaboration does not necessarily outperform either party acting alone. |

These traditions warrant caution about novelty claims. Statements that AI may weaken thinking, that critical thinking is important, that processes should be assessed or that teachers retain an essential role are not novel in isolation.

A scientific contribution must identify mechanisms, boundary conditions, patterns of interaction and empirical evidence that existing theories do not adequately explain.

### 2.2. Notable international empirical evidence

**[Bastani et al. (2025) — PNAS](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2422633122)**

An experiment involving nearly 1,000 secondary mathematics students found improvements during AI-assisted practice, but students using an unrestricted GPT interface performed worse once access to the tool was removed.

<div data-viz="bastani"></div>

**[Kestin et al. (2025) — Scientific Reports](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-025-97652-6)**

A randomised experiment with 194 Harvard physics students found that an AI tutor designed around pedagogical principles could produce better learning outcomes than in-class active learning under the study conditions.

**[Fan et al. (2025) — British Journal of Educational Technology](https://doi.org/10.1111/bjet.13544)**

Research on metacognitive laziness identifies the risk that dependence on GenAI changes self-regulated learning processes without necessarily yielding corresponding gains in competence.

**[Doshi & Hauser (2024) — Science Advances](https://doi.org/10.1126/sciadv.adn5290)**

GenAI can improve individual creative output while reducing collective diversity. This finding raises an important question for education and talent development.

**[Brynjolfsson, Li & Raymond (2025) — The Quarterly Journal of Economics](https://doi.org/10.1093/qje/qjae044)**

In a workplace study, AI increased average productivity by approximately 15%, with heterogeneous benefits across workers. This motivates hypotheses about reduced performance disparities, but does not directly establish reduced disparities in learning or competence.

### 2.3. Recent work potentially competing directly with the proposed contributions

The following 2026 references were identified as particularly important for positioning the programme. Their bibliographic details and interpretations require renewed verification before a manuscript-level novelty claim is made.

| Work | Focus already addressed | Distinctive extension to investigate |
|---|---|---|
| [Augmentation or Erosion? Regulating Cognitive Offloading in Generative AI-Supported STEM Learning](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-026-10224-6), *Educational Psychology Review*, 2026 | Allocation of cognitive activity between learners and AI as a condition of augmentation or erosion | Quantify and test internalisation, reconstruction and longer-term effects |
| [Which inference is at risk? Assessment validity reasoning and generative AI](https://doi.org/10.1080/02602938.2026.2734795), Weidlich (2026), *Assessment & Evaluation in Higher Education* | Identifying competence-related inferences threatened by AI participation in assessment | Empirically estimate diagnosticity loss and the incremental validity of alternative evidence |
| [Independent judgment before viewing generative AI recommendations reduces susceptibility to misleading advice](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2026.1936522), Yan (2026), *Frontiers in Psychology* | Sequencing independent judgment before AI recommendations in pre-service teacher education | Transfer of judgment, confidence calibration, professional development and classroom behaviour |

**Implications for research:** a further general framework asserting that cognitive effort should be preserved, authentic competence assessed or independent thinking encouraged before consulting AI is unlikely to constitute a sufficient contribution by itself. Closely related work already exists or has been identified as a potential competitor.

The priority should instead be empirical regularities that discriminate among competing theoretical explanations.
